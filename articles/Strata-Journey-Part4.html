Strata Journey Part 4: Energy, Solar, Batteries and EV Charging: From Ambition to Practical Outcomes

By GG Technology Advisory • November 4, 2026 • 6 min read

In most strata buildings, sustainability projects start with a simple question: “Should we install solar?” The reality is that the answer is rarely simple.

What initially appeared to be a straightforward project quickly became one of the longest and most complex initiatives our committee had undertaken. Before we could evaluate suppliers or prices, we first had to understand whether the building was actually ready.

Questions emerged everywhere:

  • Was the roof structurally suitable?
  • What condition was the existing electrical infrastructure in?
  • Would the cabling support future expansion?
  • How would generated electricity be measured and distributed?
  • Was there enough resident support to justify the investment?
  • Could future battery storage be incorporated?

Every answer seemed to generate three new questions.

Unlike many capital projects where the solution is relatively clear from the start, the solar journey was one of discovery. Engineers, electricians, consultants and committee members spent considerable time assessing the building’s existing infrastructure and future requirements. Discussions ranged from technical feasibility and financial returns to maintenance responsibilities, insurance implications and long-term asset management.

Perhaps the most important lesson was that successful sustainability projects are less about solar panels and more about understanding the building itself. The technology was the easy part. Building consensus, validating assumptions and ensuring the infrastructure could support both today’s requirements and tomorrow’s opportunities proved far more challenging.

The effort, however, has been worth it.

Within less than a year of operation, the solar installation generated approximately 57 MWh of electricity or 35–45 tonnes of CO₂ emissions avoided. Based on typical commercial electricity pricing, purchasing that amount of energy from the grid could have cost between $15,000 and $20,000, depending on tariffs and network charges.

For residents, the solar installation provided a visible demonstration that well-planned sustainability investments can deliver measurable results. For the committee, it reinforced an important principle: strategic projects should be evaluated not only by direct financial returns, but also by the broader resilience and future readiness they create.

The project also formed part of a wider energy strategy. Solar generation, coupled with battery storage considerations, provides opportunities to support critical building infrastructure during power outages. While no residential building can operate normally without grid power, the ability to maintain essential services such as communications, access control systems and selected common property infrastructure can significantly improve resilience during unexpected disruptions.

Most importantly, the project established a foundation for the next major challenge facing strata communities: electric vehicles.

Preparing for the EV Revolution

A few years ago, EV charging was often viewed as a future problem. Today, it is rapidly becoming a current one.

Interestingly, while our EV charging strategy was originally driven by forward planning and resident demand, recent legislative reforms have validated the importance of early preparation. New South Wales has continued to strengthen support for EV charging infrastructure within strata schemes, making it easier for owners to install chargers and limiting the ability of buildings to reject proposals without legitimate technical or safety concerns. In some cases, committees now face relatively short decision timeframes to assess EV charging requests and identify safe, compliant solutions. For buildings that have not already considered electrical capacity, metering, load management and future demand, what appears to be a simple resident request can quickly become a complex and costly infrastructure challenge.

Having already completed much of the planning, electrical assessments and infrastructure design work, our building was well positioned to respond. Rather than reacting to changing legislation, we had already established both tactical and long-term pathways for EV adoption. This proactive approach allowed the committee to focus on delivering practical solutions rather than rushing to understand the problem.

Numerous concepts, designs and technical assessments were considered. Like many existing buildings, we faced practical constraints around electrical capacity, infrastructure costs, parking arrangements, metering and future scalability. A solution that worked for a handful of vehicles today could quickly become inadequate as EV ownership increased.

The outcome was a strategy that balanced immediate needs with long-term planning.

The tactical approach focused on supporting early adopters by creating pathways for individual charging installations while maintaining safety, compliance and fair cost recovery. This enabled residents to begin transitioning to EVs without waiting for major building-wide upgrades.

The strategic approach looked further ahead. Rather than implementing ad-hoc charger installations, the committee developed a framework for future expansion, considering load management, dedicated charging infrastructure, metering arrangements and integration with renewable energy generation.

Critically, the EV strategy was never viewed as a standalone project. It was designed to leverage the solar investment already made by the building. By connecting future charging infrastructure with locally generated renewable energy, the building can improve utilisation of solar generation while reducing reliance on grid-supplied electricity.

This integrated approach transforms sustainability projects from isolated upgrades into a broader infrastructure roadmap.

Looking Beyond Cost Savings

One of the recurring lessons from both the solar and EV charging projects was that buildings often focus too heavily on immediate financial returns.

Cost savings matter, and responsible committees must carefully evaluate every investment. However, resilience, sustainability, future readiness and property attractiveness are increasingly becoming important considerations for owners and residents alike.

The world around buildings is changing. Energy costs are evolving, sustainability expectations are increasing and electric vehicles are becoming mainstream. Committees that plan ahead are not simply solving today’s problems. They are positioning their communities to adapt successfully to tomorrow’s opportunities.

The solar and EV journey required patience, technical analysis and countless discussions. Yet the outcome demonstrates what can be achieved when sustainability initiatives are approached as long-term infrastructure investments rather than short-term operational expenses.

In hindsight, the greatest value was not the electricity generated. It was creating a building better prepared for the future.
